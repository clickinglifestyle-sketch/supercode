# THE INTERVAL
## Transit

She had a four-hour connection in Amsterdam.

The flight from Philadelphia had landed at six-thirty in the morning and her connection to Warsaw did not leave until ten forty-five. She cleared passport control, found the transit area, and sat down in a row of seats near gate D8 with her bag between her feet and her laptop open.

The terminal was almost empty. In four hours it would be full — the particular density of a major international hub in the morning rush, thousands of people in transit between one place and another, the coffee queues and the gate changes and the specific human motion of a place where everyone is going somewhere else. Right now there were cleaners, a gate agent at a distant desk doing something with a computer, and a woman in her mid-fifties sitting four seats away from Vesper with a small carry-on between her feet, also not asleep.

Vesper had not slept on the crossing. She had read through her Trinity notes and written three pages she would probably cut, and then she had watched the darkness outside the window for the second half of the Atlantic.

She opened the eleven pages to the final section.

*What the Data Cannot Say.*

She had written this section last. She had known, writing it, that this was the part that would end her relationship with the journals — an environmental scientist arguing that the planet was responding, that there was something in the data consistent with a living system pushing back against a specific pressure. Not a claim about intention. Just about pattern. But it was one thing to document a human pattern across four centuries and another to say the pattern had a partner, something on the other side of it, something vastly larger and older.

She was right. She was almost certain she was right. And when it was published — when it was out, in the record, where it could be read and cited and responded to — it would change something. She believed that. It had to change something. You did not spend seven years on a thing that changed nothing.

She was also alone in an airport at four in the morning, connecting to Warsaw, on her way to a forest inside a nuclear exclusion zone, and she had not spoken to anyone — not one person, in five months — about the thing she was most certain of.

The cleaning woman reached her row.

She was perhaps sixty, with the unhurried efficiency of someone who had done this particular floor for many years and had developed an exact economy of movement — the mop going in specific arcs, no wasted passes, the machine tracking forward at a pace that covered ground without appearing to rush. She reached Vesper's row and glanced at the bag between Vesper's feet.

Vesper lifted it onto her lap.

The woman mopped under where the bag had been and moved on. At the end of the row she stopped and looked back.

"You connecting?" Dutch-accented English — the default of the airport.

"Warsaw. Then Kyiv."

The woman nodded, as though Kyiv at four in the morning was a reasonable destination. "Long trip."

"Five months," Vesper said. "I've been travelling for five months."

The woman did a quick, private calculation. "Work?"

"Research. Environmental sciences."

She looked at the laptop. At the eleven pages on the screen.

"Is it bad?" she said.

Vesper looked at her.

"What you're researching." The woman's expression was calm and direct, the face of someone who had asked because she actually wanted to know. "Is it bad news."

Vesper thought about the Somme soil. The black bags in the Fukushima fields. Ruth's mother watching the sun come up wrong. The Ferris wheel she hadn't yet seen but was going toward.

"Yes," she said. "It's mostly bad news."

The woman nodded — not defeated, just receiving it. "I grew up near here," she said. "This was farmland. My grandmother had a farm. Now it's an airport." She paused. "Not bad news, exactly. Just different." She picked up the mop. "Maybe different is not the same as bad."

She moved on to the next row.

Vesper looked at the screen. *What the Data Cannot Say.* She thought about what the cleaning woman had said, which was not the same thing as what the data said, and which was also not wrong.

She opened a new document and wrote two lines she wasn't sure were for the eleven pages or for something else, something she hadn't yet named:

*The distinction between bad news and different news depends on what you were expecting. The data expects continuation. The world has a longer memory than the data.*

She sat with that for a while.

Through the terminal windows the sky over Amsterdam was beginning to shift from black to the specific deep blue that precedes dawn. The gate agent across the terminal made an announcement in Dutch and then English about a delayed departure. Not her flight.

She took out her phone.

She had texted Eleni from the Newark departure lounge, a week ago, before she knew she was going to. *I'm connecting in Amsterdam and then heading east. I think I'm nearly done. I'm not sure of the exact day. I wanted you to know I'm coming.* Eleni had replied within minutes: *The tea will be ready.*

She read that exchange again. Then she put her phone away.

She thought about Elathon. The upper grove, the edge trees, still holding in the last photographs she had seen. There was still time. She would be home in two weeks. Finish Chernobyl, write the last section on the flight out, submit from Thessaloniki before she got on the train south. The document would already be moving through the system by the time she arrived. She would get there before winter properly closed in.

She went back to the eleven pages and kept reading. Around her the terminal was still and quiet, the cleaning woman's machine moving down a distant corridor now, the gate agent stretching at his desk, the woman four seats away with her eyes closed.

The sky outside continued its slow transaction from dark to light.

She read until boarding.
