# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Seventeen

She knew the numbers. She had built the numbers. She could locate herself in them the way a navigator locates a ship on a chart — not with certainty, but with enough information to know the general territory.

She was thirty-nine years old. She was in the Peloponnese, in a village on the front line of Mediterranean climate change — the projections for this region were among the most severe in Europe, the heat events, the drought cycles, the wildfire risk, the agricultural collapse already underway in the soil beneath her. She had no institutional position, no salary, no safety net beyond what she had saved across eleven years of contracts and commissions. She had refused the one offer that would have given her the resources and the access and the protection of the most powerful governments on earth.

She had come home to a dying place and chosen to stay in it.

She sat with all of this in the kitchen one morning, the grove visible in the window, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold without her noticing.

---

The people most likely to die in what was coming: she knew the profile. The Global South. The poor. The old. The young. The people without resources or institutional connections or the mobility to move when the conditions became unsurvivable. The people who contributed least to producing the problem and had the least capacity to respond to it.

She did not fit this profile. She was educated, mobile, connected to the international research community. She had no dependants, no debts, no obligations that could not be renegotiated. She could go somewhere safer — the north of Europe, the highlands of places that the models showed as more stable across the century. She had the means to do it. She had just chosen not to.

The data said she was more likely to survive than to be among the toll. Statistically. In aggregate. At the scale at which the data operated.

The data had never operated at the scale of one person. She had always known this and had always, in her work, treated it as a limitation of the instrument rather than a problem for her own life. Now it was a problem for her own life.

She could not tell from the data whether she would be among the survivors or the victims. She could tell it was probably the former. She could not tell what surviving would cost.

---

She thought about what surviving meant.

The grove had perhaps five years. The contamination in the lower soil was spreading at the rate her models predicted, which was to say it was spreading at the rate the interventions had accelerated it, which was to say that what had been done to help was doing what the things done to help always did. In five years, or seven, or ten — the upper grove would be gone. The oldest tree would be gone.

The village would not be gone. People would stay, or some would stay, or the ones who could leave would leave and the ones who couldn't would find a way to be here in the changed thing. She had seen this in Beirut and in St. George, Utah and in the villages outside the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone. People stayed in places that had been irreparably damaged. They found a way to be present to what was left.

She would probably be one of them. Statistically, probably.

But she had understood, since she sat in Eleni's kitchen and heard November and the seventh, that surviving in body was not the same as surviving in full. Andreas had survived his illness right up until he didn't. The grove was surviving right now, this morning, the oldest tree still alive. The upper branches catching the light that came through the kitchen window at this hour — she could see it from where she sat, the same light from the same angle she had been watching for three weeks, learning the pattern of it.

You could survive while losing the things that made survival the point. She had done this for eleven years without knowing it — surviving in Thessaloniki, producing the work, building the case, and losing the grove and Andreas and the ordinary life she had not allowed herself to want directly enough to choose.

She was here now. She was in the grove and in the house and in the village and she was not going back. Whatever surviving looked like from here, it would be this — present to the place as it changed, as it contracted, as the trees died one by one and the soil gave up its last chemistry and the village became something different from what it had been. She would be here for it.

Whether that was surviving or being a victim of a slower kind, she genuinely did not know.

---

She looked at her hands.

She had pressed them against things throughout the past year — stone, soil, bark. The instruments of the kind of knowledge the data did not contain. The knowledge of the actual weight of something, the actual temperature of it, the actual roughness of a surface that had been shaped over centuries. Her hands had been telling her things the data could not tell her, and she had been learning, slowly, to listen.

She looked at them now — the specific hands she had, their particular shape, the cold in them from holding the cup — and thought about what they would do, where they would go, what they would touch in the years she had.

She did not know.

She had spent eleven years knowing things. She had built the methodology to know them properly, to know them in the terms the consensus would have to accept, to know them in ways that could not be dismissed. She had known the pattern and the root and the mechanism and the toll and the trajectory. She had given the numbers to six people on a screen and she had written the letter that refused the position and she had heard the tape in the grove.

She was at the end of what knowing could do for her.

What came next was not knowing. What came next was being here, in the uncertainty, without the instrument she had spent her whole career building. The data had taken her as far as the data could go. The rest was something else — the thing Andreas had been trying to tell her from the excavation trench, the thing Haddad had asked her about in the Beirut café, the thing Mori had named when she asked what would change her mind.

The rest was living in the place, with the data she had and the uncertainty she had and the hands she had and the time she had — however much that was.

---

She thought about Haddad, in Beirut. About Mori in Hiroshima. About Ruth in Utah, still filing, still testifying, forty years in. About Viktor watching the wolves in the Exclusion Zone.

They had the document. They would do what they would do with it. She had not told them what to do. She would not. Whatever grew from the four copies she had sent — if anything grew — it would grow in its own direction, in the intervals between the mobilisations, in the spaces the pattern had not yet consumed. She could not manage it. She could not direct it. She could only have sent it, and she had.

She thought about Eleni bringing food every day without being asked. About the village treating her return with the quiet care of people who do not say directly what they mean. About the oldest tree, still alive this morning, the light on it through the window.

She thought about what she would do today. Not the year, not the decade, not the trajectory. Today.

She would go to the grove. She would put her hand on the oldest tree. She would pay attention to what was there. She would come back and eat something and perhaps write something — not for a journal, not for a consensus, just because the record mattered and she was the person who kept it. She would talk to Eleni if Eleni came. She would not plan further than that.

---

She did not know if she would be among the survivors or the victims of what was coming. She knew the probabilities. She knew they told her nothing certain. She knew she had chosen a place that was already dying and would continue to die and that she had chosen it with full knowledge, which was perhaps the only kind of choice she had ever trusted.

She knew that two to three billion people — mostly not like her, mostly in places that could not afford her uncertainty as a philosophical position because the uncertainty was already killing them — would bear the weight of the century ahead in ways she would witness but not share equally. She knew that this was unjust and that the injustice was structural and that naming it did not correct it.

She knew that the oldest tree in the grove in Elathon was still alive this morning.

She knew that Andreas had put his hand on it and thought of her.

She knew that she was here, finally, in the place that was hers, with her hands available for whatever the place needed from them.

She got up. She put on her coat. She left the cold tea on the table and went outside into the January morning — the air sharp and clear, the light coming in low over the ridge, the grove visible from the path, some of the trees still holding, some already gone, the line between them moving slowly southward, the oldest tree standing at the highest point where it had always stood.

She walked up to it.

She put both hands flat against the bark.

She did not know what was coming. She was here for it anyway.

The morning continued. The light moved. The tree was cold and real beneath her palms.

Somewhere in the grove, the bird she had been hearing for three weeks — the one she had not yet managed to see — began again.

She closed her eyes.

She listened.
