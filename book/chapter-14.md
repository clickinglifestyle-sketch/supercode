# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Fourteen

The contact came through Brandt.

An email, eight days after the rejection, careful in its language in the way of someone who had been told to be careful. Professor Brandt had shared the document — she was not apologising for this, the email made clear — with colleagues in the policy architecture she had spent thirty years working within. The document had been read. There were people who wished to speak with her. The conversation would be private. No record would be kept, which she could take to mean whatever she wished.

Vesper read the email in Eleni's kitchen and looked out the window at the upper grove for a long time.

Then she wrote back: she would not travel. If they wished to speak with her they could do so from where she was. She would give them one hour.

The reply came within twenty minutes. They accepted her terms.

---

She took the call at the kitchen table, her laptop open, the window behind her framing the grove in the pale January light. She had not thought about what would be visible behind her. When the screen filled with faces she watched two of them notice the window and what was in it.

Six people. She had not been told who they were and did not ask. She could identify three of them from their faces — a senior scientific advisor to the European Commission whom she recognised from conference photographs, a woman she believed was a deputy national security advisor to the current American administration, and a man whose face she knew from news coverage as the head of the UN Secretary-General's office on climate risk. The other three she did not recognise.

And one more, joining several minutes after the others, whose face she recognised without having expected to see it here: the leader of a country she had written about extensively, whose agricultural programs had contributed directly to the collapse she had been documenting for seven years. The leader looked older than the photographs. They looked like someone who had not been sleeping well.

No one introduced themselves by title. Brandt, off-screen, had arranged this. They were here as people, not positions.

The European Commission advisor spoke first. "You understand why we could not allow the document to circulate."

"Yes," Vesper said. "I understood before I submitted it."

"Then why submit it?"

"Because the record should exist. Suppressed is not the same as gone."

A silence. The leader whose country she had written about looked at the grove through the window and said nothing.

---

"We need the numbers," the American said. "The real numbers. Not the official projections."

"The official projections are technically accurate within their assumptions," Vesper said. "The assumptions exclude the compounding effects of the pattern I've documented. If you remove those exclusions—"

"That's what we need," the American said. "Remove the exclusions."

Vesper looked at the screen for a moment. She had thought about how to say this for seven years. She had written it in the document and the document had been suppressed. She said it now, to six people in a secure call from a kitchen in the Peloponnese, with the dying grove behind her.

"The soil," she said. "First. A third of viable topsoil globally is already degraded beyond recovery at any timescale relevant to the current food system. At current rates of loss, the major agricultural regions — the American grain belt, the Indo-Gangetic plain, the North China Plain, the sub-Saharan growing regions — face yield reductions that the official models project at ten to twenty percent. Those projections assume continued successful technological intervention. If you apply the pattern I've documented — each intervention generating consequences that require a larger intervention, compounding over decades — the realistic figure for several of those regions is forty percent or higher. In some, it approaches total collapse."

No one spoke.

"A forty percent reduction in global food production," Vesper said, "applied to a population of eight billion people, the majority of whom have no surplus to absorb that reduction, produces famine at a scale not seen since before the agricultural era. The historical record of famine mortality as a percentage of affected population gives you a range. Applied to the affected populations in the realistic scenario — primarily the Global South, primarily the poor, primarily children — the cumulative mortality over the century runs into the hundreds of millions from food collapse alone."

The UN official said: "The models show—"

"The models show what the models are designed to show," Vesper said. She said it without harshness. "I have been documenting what the models exclude for eleven years. If you want the numbers that include what the models exclude, I can give you those. That's why you're here."

The UN official was quiet.

"Continue," the European advisor said.

"Water stress, compounding with food stress: two billion people currently without reliable access. Five billion projected by mid-century. Not as an independent variable — as a compounding one. Food insecurity and water insecurity in the same populations at the same time produce displacement and conflict that the mortality models account for separately and incorrectly. The conflicts generated by resource competition on a planet where the major resources are contracting are not like the conflicts of the twentieth century. They are not containable by the security architectures built for the twentieth century."

She paused.

"The nuclear dimension. I will not model it in detail because the uncertainty ranges are too wide. I will say: the arms control architecture built after the Cold War is largely dismantled. Nine states with operational nuclear weapons. The most likely exchange scenario — a regional conflict between two nuclear-armed states under severe resource and climate stress — would kill fifty to a hundred million people in the initial exchange and trigger atmospheric effects that would reduce agricultural yields globally by thirty to forty percent for a period of years. In a world already at the margins of food security, you understand what that means."

The room was very quiet. Through the window the grove was lit by the pale winter sun. One of the living trees caught the light for a moment and held it.

"Pandemic," Vesper said. "At current rates of habitat destruction and narrowing of the interval between human civilisation and wild ecosystems — I have documented this pattern as clearly as the agricultural pattern — the frequency and severity of spillover events will continue to increase. COVID-19 killed between seven and twenty million people directly, depending on methodology, in a world that had functioning healthcare systems and resources to mobilise a response. The next major spillover event will occur in a world where those systems are under significantly greater stress. The mortality multiplier is not linear."

She looked at the screen.

"Add these together. Apply them across the century. Use conservative assumptions for each individual variable and compound them, which is what the real world does. The cumulative toll — deaths above the baseline projection that would not occur in the absence of the pattern I have documented — is in the range of two to three billion people. Concentrated in the second half of the century. Concentrated in the Global South, in the poor, in children. Unevenly distributed in the way that all the consequences in my work are unevenly distributed: falling hardest on the people who contributed least to producing them and have the least institutional capacity to respond."

The leader whose country she had written about, who had been silent throughout, said: "Two to three billion."

"That is the range under conservative assumptions with the pattern holding," Vesper said. "If the feedback loops trigger at the higher end — and several of them are already showing early indicators — the number is higher."

---

The silence lasted long enough that she thought the connection had failed.

Then the European advisor said: "Is there anything that changes this trajectory?"

She had known this question was coming. She had been sitting with it since Chernobyl, since the grove, since she had pressed her palm to the oldest tree and understood what she understood.

"Not in the terms you're asking," she said. "There is no program, no policy, no technology, no institution that addresses the root. Every intervention you can design will be an expression of the same mechanism that produced the problem. I have the data for this across seventeen case studies and four centuries. The interventions are not wrong individually. They are structurally incapable of addressing a structural condition. This is not a criticism of the people who design them. It is a description of what the pattern does."

"Then what is there?" the American said. The voice had changed — quieter, the official manner gone. Just a person asking.

"There is the interval," Vesper said. "There is what happens in the spaces the pattern has not yet consumed. The people who are already living differently inside it — not because they have a theory about it, but because the pattern came for them and they found a way to stay present to the place they were in and keep going." She paused. "The Chernobyl Exclusion Zone. Thirty-eight years of human absence and the ecosystem response has been more significant than any remediation program in the documented record. The planet is self-regulating. It has been doing so for 3.5 billion years. What interrupts the recovery is not the radiation — it's the return of the human pressure that generated the original damage. When the pressure recedes, the system responds."

"You're saying we should do nothing," the UN official said.

"I'm saying that doing nothing is not the same as the absence of the mobilisation logic. Hanna in her garden in the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone is not doing nothing. She is present. She is attentive. She is not extracting. She is not directing resources at defined objectives. She is simply in the place that is hers, paying attention to it, for as long as she can." Vesper looked at the screen. "I cannot recommend this as policy. I cannot tell you to implement it. It cannot be implemented. It can only be done, by people, one at a time, in the places that are theirs."

The leader whose country she had written about said: "That will not save two billion people."

"No," Vesper said. "It will not."

"Then what is the point of it?"

She looked at the window. At the grove. At the oldest tree, visible from here, the one that had been alive for longer than any institution any of them had ever worked for.

"The point of it," she said, "is that it is true. It is the only thing I can tell you that is both true and not another expression of the pattern. Everything else I could say — every program, every target, every coordinated global response — I can tell you exactly what it will produce, because I have the data for every version of it that has been tried. This I cannot tell you what it will produce, because it is not a program. It is a way of being present to the place you are in. The data does not cover it. It is outside the data."

The leader was quiet for a long time.

Then: "What should I do. Not as—" A pause. "As a person."

Vesper looked at the face on the screen. The exhaustion in it. The specific exhaustion of someone who has been carrying the weight of the calculation for a long time.

"Find the place that is yours," she said. "Go there. Stay. Pay attention to it. Don't try to fix it. Just be there, for as long as you can be."

She said it as simply as she could. She did not say: I know this is not enough. She did not say: I know you came here for something larger. She did not say: I know what it costs to hear this from a woman sitting in a dying grove in the Peloponnese.

They all knew.

---

The call ended.

She closed the laptop and sat in the kitchen for a while, with the grove in the window and the food Eleni had left on the counter and the specific silence of a house that was beginning, slowly, to be inhabited again.

Then she put on her coat and went outside.

The oldest tree was where it had always been. The light on it was the light of a January afternoon in the Peloponnese, low and pale and very clear. She walked to it and put her hand on the bark and stood there.

Two to three billion. She had said it plainly, without flinching, in the register of data, which was the only register she had ever fully trusted. She had built the methodology across seven years to be able to say it and have it mean something. It meant something. It would be heard by six people and suppressed and it would mean something.

Somewhere in Beirut, Haddad had the document. In Hiroshima, Mori. In Utah, Ruth. In the Exclusion Zone, Dmytro.

The world would continue in the direction it was going. The pattern would continue. The toll would accumulate in the way that tolls accumulate — gradually, unevenly, out of sight of the people and institutions with the power to name it officially.

And in the intervals, the things that were not the pattern would continue too.

The forests in the Exclusion Zone. The old women in their gardens. The surveys completed and handed to archives. The people who came home.

She stayed until she could no longer feel her hand for cold.

Then she pressed it harder against the bark, one more time, and felt the tree.

Then she went in.
