# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Sixteen

Eleni put it on the kitchen table four days after the meal, after the refusal, after the conversation about Ambrose and the commission.

A cassette tape in a plastic case, the kind that had stopped being made in most of the world before Vesper left for Athens the first time. The label on the spine was in his handwriting. Her name. Just her name. Nothing else.

"He gave it to me in September," Eleni said. "When he submitted the survey. He said give it to her when she comes back. He said you would come back." She paused. "He was not always right about everything. He was right about that."

She put her coat on and went home and left Vesper alone in the kitchen with the tape.

---

She did not have anything to play it on.

She sat with it for an hour before she thought to look in the back of the cabinet where her parents had kept things they no longer used but could not bring themselves to discard. The player was there — a small portable cassette deck, the kind she had used as a child to record herself reading, to make gifts for grandparents. The batteries were dead. She found batteries in a drawer.

She carried the player to the grove.

---

She sat at the base of the oldest tree. She put the player on the ground beside her. She looked at the tape for a while.

Then she pressed play.

---

*There's a thing I've been trying to say to you for about twenty years,* he said. *I thought I'd try saying it to a tape and see if that helps.*

His voice was exactly as she remembered it and entirely different from how she remembered it — the same register, the same particular quality of unhurried attention, but older, the way voices get older while you're not listening to them.

*I'm going to talk about a few things. The grove. Your work. The stone in the trench. In whatever order they come. You can skip the parts you already know.*

---

The grove first.

*I've been watching it die for seven years from the ground,* he said. *Not the satellite view — I've seen your maps, I know what those show. I mean from here. From the root systems. The way the soil feels different when you dig into it, the moisture wrong, the colour wrong, the smell of something that shouldn't be breaking down, breaking down. I've been documenting it for the survey but there are things you document and things you just know, and what I know from being in this soil is that the land is responding to something. Not just the programs. Something bigger. I don't have your language for it. I don't have the data. But I've been standing in this grove for thirty years and I know when a place is being pushed past what it can hold.*

A pause. Wind in the recording, or the movement of his hands.

*I think you know it too. I think you've known it longer than anyone. I think that's why you left.*

---

She stopped the tape.

She sat with that for a while. The grove was quiet around her. The oldest tree behind her, its bark against her spine.

She pressed play.

---

*I've read both volumes,* he said. *And the responses. The methodological objections. I've read all of it, probably twice. You're right. I want you to know that I think you're right — not as a compliment, not to make you feel better about the rejection. Because I've been standing in the place the data describes and the data is describing the place. The soil of the Peloponnese is telling me the same thing your satellite images are telling you. The pattern is real. The cause is real. And the thing you wrote in the document Eleni showed me — the thing about the planet responding — I knew that before you had the framework for it. I just knew it from the smell of the soil.*

He paused.

*I think that's what you needed the data for. Not to convince yourself. To convince the people who weren't standing in the place. The people who only believe what they can measure from a distance. You needed the data because you were trying to reach people who don't have the soil under their hands. I understand that. I just want you to know that from here, from the ground, it was already obvious. You were right from the beginning.*

---

She pressed stop again.

She was not crying. She was sitting very still against the oldest tree with the cassette player in her hands and the pale January light moving through the upper branches and something happening in her chest that she did not have a word for that was not grief and was not relief and was both of those things and neither.

She pressed play.

---

*What I wished,* he said, and then stopped for a moment. *What I wished — and I'm going to say this plainly, because I've run out of time for saying things in the way that leaves room for interpretation — is that we could have faced this together. Not fixing it. I know it can't be fixed from the outside. I mean just — been in the same place, at the same time, knowing what was coming. You doing what you do and me doing what I do and both of us knowing that the thing we loved was dying, and being here while it was still here.*

He paused.

*I know why that didn't happen. I'm not saying it as an accusation. You needed the distance. The distance was how you knew things. I understood that. I just — I kept hoping that eventually the distance would have given you what you needed and you'd be ready to come back. I kept thinking there was still time.*

A long pause.

*There wasn't quite enough. But you'll come back anyway. I know you. You'll come back and the grove will still have some of it left and you'll put your hand on the oldest tree and you'll know the thing I've been trying to tell you.*

---

She stopped the tape.

She put her hand flat against the bark of the oldest tree without deciding to.

---

She let it run to the end.

*The afternoon at the dig,* he said, near the end. *I've thought about it many times. I think you felt the stone. Not what it meant historically — I mean you felt it. The weight of it, the cold of it, the person who cut it. I think that was the moment you could have gone either way — stayed in the analysis or come down into it with me. I don't blame you for going back to the analysis. It was what you knew how to do. It was what you were building. But I've always thought — if you'd let yourself feel it without immediately making it a finding, you might have found your way back here a little sooner.*

*Maybe not. Maybe you needed all of it. The years, the data, the working groups. Maybe you needed to build the whole case before you could trust what you already knew in your hands.*

A pause. The sound of him breathing.

*Anyway. The upper grove is still alive. Some of it. When you come back — go to the oldest tree. Put your hand on it the way you put your hand on the stone in the trench. Don't analyse it. Just feel it.*

*That's what I've been trying to tell you for twenty years. That's all I ever wanted for you.*

*Come home when you can. I think you're almost ready.*

The tape ran on for a few seconds of silence. Then it ended.

---

She sat in the grove for a long time.

The light moved through the branches in the way it moved in January — low, specific, the shadows long and clear. In the upper section one of the living trees caught the sun briefly and held it, the leaves going gold for a moment before the light shifted.

She had built eleven years of work into a document that said what he had known from the smell of the soil. She had traveled to six locations across the world to document what he had been standing inside for thirty years. She had needed the data because she did not trust what she already knew in her hands. He had understood this about her. He had loved her anyway.

She pressed her palm flat against the bark.

Not analyzing. Not documenting. Not making it a finding.

Just feeling it — the rough surface, the cold, the specific weight of something that had been putting its roots down into this ground for longer than any record she had ever found. The tree that had been alive when the soil beneath it was healthy and was still alive now, barely, its roots still working, still pulling what it could from the ground that was failing it.

Still here. Still trying. Not because it had a theory about what was happening. Because it was in the place that was its place and staying was what it knew how to do.

She kept her hand on the bark until she couldn't feel it for cold.

Then she kept it there anyway.

Above her, in the living branches of the oldest tree in the grove in Elathon, a bird she couldn't see began to sing.

She listened.

She did not move.

She did not take notes.
