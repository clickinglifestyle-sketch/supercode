# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Eleven

The train south from Thessaloniki took four hours.

She had spent one night in her apartment — long enough to collect what she needed, to stand for a moment in the rooms she had been working from for three years, to look at the desk with its stacked documents and the window that faced the other building's wall and understand, in the specific way she understood things now, that she had confused this place for a life.

She got on the train in the morning.

The landscape changed as the train moved south — the flat agricultural land of the north giving way to hills, then to the particular topography of the Peloponnese: steep and rocky, the villages clinging to the slopes, the valleys narrow and deep. She had been watching this landscape on a satellite map for seven years. She had not come back in eleven.

The olive trees began appearing at the edge of the valley floors. Grey ones. Not the grey of winter — she knew the grey of winter, the way the leaves went silver-pale and came back in spring. This was different. This was the grey of something that had stopped.

She looked at them for a long time from the train window.

She did not write in her notebook.

---

Eleni's house was at the upper end of the village, a fifteen-minute walk from the bus stop. Vesper had called ahead from Thessaloniki. When she knocked, the door opened immediately, and Eleni was standing in it — older than she remembered, which was always the way, and with the specific expression of someone who has been waiting longer than the walk from the kitchen.

"Come in," Eleni said. "Tea is already made."

---

The kitchen was warm. The window looked out over the slope toward the upper grove. Vesper sat at the table and wrapped her hands around the cup and looked at the window and then looked at Eleni, who sat across from her and was quiet in the way of someone who knows what they have to say and is deciding how to say it.

"Tell me," Vesper said.

Eleni looked at her cup. "November," she said. "The seventh."

She said the word she had been not-saying. "Ambrose."

"Yes."

Vesper looked at the window. The upper grove was visible from here — the tree line at the top of the slope, some of the crowns still holding their leaves, some bare, the line between them invisible from this distance but present.

"What happened?" she said.

"His heart." Eleni paused. "He had known for some time. He didn't tell many people."

"He didn't tell me."

Eleni looked at her without saying what they both knew, which was that Ambrose had tried.

---

*He had texted her in November. She had seen it on the train between Thessaloniki and Athens, read it twice, and put her phone face-down on the seat beside her.*

*The text said: I think about that afternoon at the dig sometimes. The stone. I think you felt something you didn't say.*

*She had looked out the window at the landscape going past. She had thought about what to write back for forty minutes.*

*Then the train arrived at Athens.*

*She was in Nevada on November seventh. She had picked up the phone in the dark of a hotel room in Albuquerque and held it and put it down and gone to sleep.*

*She did the arithmetic now, sitting in Eleni's kitchen. She did it very quickly. She had been doing it since the phone call in Kyiv, in the part of her mind that worked on things she wasn't ready to look at directly.*

*The arithmetic came out the same each time.*

---

They sat for a while. Eleni refilled the tea without being asked.

Outside, a bird moved through the upper grove — she could see it from the window, a quick dark shape in the branches of one of the trees that still had leaves.

"He finished the survey," Eleni said eventually. "Professor Makris's survey. He finished the documentation of the whole site. Submitted it to the archive in Patras in September." She paused. "He asked me to make sure you knew."

Vesper looked at the table.

He had spent the last years of his life completing the record of a place that had been inhabited for three thousand years, whose soil was now being destroyed by the same mechanism she had been documenting across the world, whose ancient trees were dying at the rate she had been tracking on a satellite map while he sent her a text about a stone she had touched in a trench in September light and she had not answered.

He had finished the documentation. He had asked Eleni to make sure she knew.

She did not cry. She sat with it.

"Can I see the grove?" she said.

---

They walked up together as the afternoon light was going — Eleni knowing the path without looking at it, Vesper putting one foot in front of the other on ground she had not walked on in eleven years and that her body remembered anyway, the muscle memory of a childhood of this exact slope.

The lower grove was gone. She had known this from the satellite images, but the images had not prepared her for the specific quality of it — not the dramatic fallen-tree catastrophe she might have imagined, but something quieter and more final: the trees standing, still upright, utterly still, their branches bare in a way that was not seasonal. The soil at their roots cracked and pale. The silence underneath them absolute in a way that was different from the silence of living trees.

She stopped at the edge of the lower grove and stood for a moment.

Then she looked up toward the upper grove.

The line was visible here, close up — the trees that were holding and the trees that were not, the distinction not dramatic, not a clear boundary, but a gradual fading, the last living trees growing less certain of themselves as you moved south toward the dead ones. Some of the upper grove's trees were bare like the lower. Others still held their leaves — grey-green, thinned, the canopy less dense than she remembered, but present.

Some of the trees her grandmother had known were still alive.

Not many. But some.

She walked to the nearest living tree and put her hand against its trunk.

The bark was rough and ridged, the specific texture of old olive bark — nothing like the excavation stone, nothing like the Somme soil or Hanna's vegetable bed, entirely its own thing, a surface she had not touched since she was a child.

She stood there with her hand on the tree.

Eleni waited at a slight distance, which was the right thing to do.

The light was going. The air was cold in the way of a December evening in the Peloponnese — not severe, just present, the sea somewhere below in the valley carrying its particular cold. From the upper slope she could see, in the remaining light, the shape of the village below, the road she had walked up, the roofline of Eleni's house, and beyond it the descending slope toward the valley where the road wound back toward the train station, toward Thessaloniki, toward the airports she had been departing from for five months.

She was not going back.

She did not decide this in the way she made decisions. She simply understood it as true, the way you understand something that was already true before you named it.

She kept her hand on the tree. The bark was cool and rough and real in a way that her notebook and her data and her eleven pages — all of it accurate, all of it carefully documented — were not quite, in the same way.

Behind her, Eleni said nothing.

The bird she had seen from the kitchen window was somewhere in the upper grove. She could hear it, intermittent, in the deepening dark.

She stayed.
