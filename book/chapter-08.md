# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Eight

She arrived in Hiroshima on a Tuesday, in rain.

The city received her the way modern Japanese cities received arrivals — efficiently, cleanly, the tram from the station moving through wide streets past department stores and office buildings and a covered shopping arcade that stretched for several blocks. She had been told, before she left Berlin, that this was the most disorienting thing about Hiroshima: that it was a normal city. That you could spend a day in it and not know.

The A-Bomb Dome was visible from the tram.

She had seen it in photographs many times. The industrial exhibition hall that had been the only structure near the hypocenter to remain partially standing after the bomb — its distinctive domed frame still upright, the walls still holding, the interior burned out and open to the sky. The city had been rebuilt around it over seventy years and now it stood at the edge of the Peace Memorial Park, in the centre of the modern city, surrounded by trees and the river and the sounds of trams and traffic.

She stood on the bank of the Motoyasu River and looked at it for a long time.

The decision to preserve it had not been simple — there had been arguments for demolition, for leaving the ruins of the past behind and building forward. The preservation was a choice made against another choice, in a city that had had to decide, very deliberately, what it would carry and what it would set down.

She thought about the Humboldt Forum. She thought about the murals on the East Side Gallery, the original Wall buried underneath.

What you choose to keep and what you choose to rebuild. The choices are always made in the same language.

---

She spent four hours in the Peace Memorial Museum.

She had prepared herself — she was a historian, she had read the records, she knew what the museum contained. The preparation did not help. The testimony of the hibakusha, the medical documentation, the personal effects — a child's lunchbox, a girl's school uniform, a watch stopped at 8:15 — were not data in the way she used data. They were specific. Each one was irreducibly itself.

She took almost no notes.

At the end of the permanent exhibition she sat for a long time on a bench. Around her other visitors moved through the space — a school group, their teacher speaking in a low voice, the students quiet in the way of young people encountering something that exceeds the frameworks they've been given. A European couple, one of them weeping quietly. An older Japanese man standing in front of a particular case for a very long time.

She thought about the eleven pages.

She thought about the word *propensity.* Her word. A clean, analytical word that meant the tendency of the human organism toward organised destruction. The lunchbox in the case did not use this word. The girl who had carried it had not been an expression of a propensity. She had been a specific person eating a specific lunch at a specific moment when a thing happened that was caused by human decisions that were themselves caused by other human decisions that went back, if you traced them far enough, to something Vesper had spent seven years trying to name.

She sat with this for a while. Then she left the museum and walked into the park in the rain.

---

Professor Mori worked at Hiroshima City University, in an office filled not with books but with boxes of recorded testimony — cassette tapes, reel-to-reel, digital drives, transcripts in Japanese and English and several other languages. She had spent twenty years collecting the oral histories of atomic bomb survivors. Most of them were gone now. She was working to archive what remained before the last of the hibakusha generation died.

She had read Vesper's work. She had agreed to meet her.

She was a small woman in her early sixties, with the quiet manner of someone who had spent two decades listening rather than speaking. She poured tea. She asked about the journey from Berlin. And then, without transition, she said:

"What would change your mind?"

Vesper looked at her.

"Your hypothesis," Mori said. "You have been building it for seven years. The data goes in one direction. What would you need to see to conclude that you were wrong? That the pattern does not hold? That some interventions do stop the propensity rather than feeding it?"

Vesper thought about it honestly.

"If the data showed — across sufficient sample sizes, with the confounding variables controlled — that a sustained mobilization had resolved the conditions that generated it, without producing a larger problem in response. If the outcomes of intervention were, on balance and over time, better than the counterfactual."

"And has any evidence moved you in that direction?"

"Some cases are ambiguous. The post-war Japanese economic recovery is the strongest counter-argument. The outcomes were—"

"I am not asking about the outcomes," Mori said. "I am asking whether anything has changed your mind. Whether the hypothesis is a conclusion you reached or a conclusion you are building toward." She looked at Vesper over her tea. "The difference matters to the science."

The room was quiet. Rain against the window.

"I think I reached it," Vesper said. "And then spent seven years building the evidence. And I believe the evidence supports it. But I understand what you're asking."

"What I am asking," Mori said, "is whether you came to Hiroshima to find something, or to confirm something."

Vesper didn't answer. The honest answer was both. The more honest answer was that she was no longer certain she could tell the difference.

"You are making Hiroshima into an example," Mori said. Not unkindly. "Into a case study in an argument about the human propensity for destruction. I understand why. The argument is serious and the evidence is real." She set down her tea. "But the people whose testimony is in those boxes were not expressions of a propensity. They were people. And some of them spent the rest of their lives arguing for nuclear abolition, not because they had a theory about human nature but because they had lived through it and they could not stop trying to prevent it happening again." She paused. "They mobilized. They campaigned. They testified, year after year, to anyone who would listen. By your framework, this mobilization fed the pattern. By their experience, it was the only honest response to what they had seen."

Vesper said nothing.

"I do not say your framework is wrong," Mori said. "I say it is incomplete. It can tell you what the pattern does. It cannot tell you what a person should do who is standing inside it."

---

She went to Fukushima the following week.

The exclusion zone had contracted over the years — some towns decontaminated, evacuation orders lifted, residents encouraged to return. Return rates were low. The towns that had been reopened had the quality of places that had been cleaned but not inhabited — the infrastructure functional, the streets quiet, the shops few and the residents mostly elderly. The young families had not come back.

In the fields outside one such town she saw the bags.

Millions of them — black, cubic, stacked in rows across the agricultural land. Each one contained contaminated topsoil, removed during the decontamination, stored here while the question of what to do with it remained unresolved. From the road they covered the landscape in every direction, black against the pale autumn fields, a new geography laid over the old one.

A worker from the decontamination authority gave her the figures: 14 million cubic metres of contaminated soil. Current storage capacity sufficient for the next several years. Permanent storage site not yet determined. The bags were supposed to be temporary. That had been a decade ago.

She wrote in her notebook: *The documentation is complete. The bags are labeled. The volume is measured. The solution does not exist. The same gap as the Beirut port. The same gap as the Somme shells. The gap between knowing and the capacity to act on what is known.*

She looked at the bags for a long time.

Then she wrote: *Hiroshima to Fukushima: the same technology, renamed. The bomb that destroyed the city became the reactor that powered the recovery. The peaceful atom — the propensity redirected, rebranded, offered back as progress. And the reactor failed and the soil was poisoned and the bags are stacked in the fields and the solution does not exist and the propensity continues in the next direction.*

*The miracle is not the interruption of the pattern. The miracle is the pattern, wearing different clothes.*

She stood at the edge of the field and thought about the specific girl's lunchbox in the museum in Hiroshima — the lunch her mother had packed that morning, the ordinary Tuesday of it, the 8:14 before the 8:15.

She thought about Mori's question: *what would change your mind?*

She did not have a better answer now than she'd had in Hiroshima. The data was the data. The pattern held.

But something in the question had stayed with her — not the methodological challenge, but the thing underneath it. The hibakusha who had spent fifty years testifying. Who had mobilized, by Vesper's framework, in exactly the wrong direction. Who had done it anyway, because they were standing inside what had happened and they could not do nothing.

She could not call that wrong.

She didn't know what to call it.

She opened her phone. She went to the Elathon community page.

The most recent post was from three days ago. A photograph of the lower grove: the trees almost entirely grey now, the leaves small and curled, a few of them still holding on at the very ends of the outermost branches. Someone had written beneath it: *We are losing them.*

Four words. Forty-seven likes.

She stood among the black bags in the pale Fukushima afternoon and looked at the photograph of the trees she had grown up beneath and had not returned to in eleven years and the word she had been not-using for a long time came up directly: *Ambrose.*

She closed the page.

She got back in the car.

She did not call.
