# THE INTERVAL
## Chapter Twelve

She had sent the eleven pages from Thessaloniki, the night before she got on the train. A journal in London that had published her second volume and rejected her third submission with the now-familiar methodological objections. She had written a cover letter of one paragraph, described the work accurately, closed with *I believe the evidence is sufficient.* Sent it. Gone to sleep.

The chorus would respond or it wouldn't. She had stopped being the person whose life depended on which.

---

She woke in her parents' house — hers now, had been for years, the inheritance she hadn't known what to do with and had left in the care of a neighbour who checked on it and aired it out in summer. The bedroom she had grown up in. The ceiling she had looked at as a child, the specific quality of the light through the east window in the early morning, the sound of the village below beginning its day.

She lay for a while and listened.

Then she got up, put on her coat, and went to the grove.

---

The upper grove in the morning.

Some of the trees still held their leaves — less than she had hoped, more than she had feared. The living ones looked tired, the way trees looked when something in the soil was working against them, but they were alive, the roots still pulling whatever they could from ground that was not yet fully gone.

She walked in among them.

She had not walked here since she was twenty-eight. She had been the kind of person who kept precise records of everywhere she had been and had not included this in them for eleven years, which told her something she had not wanted to look at directly.

The ground under the trees was the ground of her childhood. The particular quality of olive grove soil — dense with fallen leaves over centuries, dark, the tree roots visible at the surface in places, the ground uneven around the oldest trunks. She knew where to put her feet. She had not known she still knew.

She walked to the oldest tree in the upper grove. It had been old when her grandmother was young, old when her grandmother's grandmother was young, the trunk so thick and ridged it looked like it had been wrung by hand over centuries, the wood grey and dense and entirely patient.

She put her hand on it.

She stood there and waited for the urgency she had been carrying for five months — for longer than that, if she was honest, for years — and it was not there. She did not know what to do with its absence. She was not sure she had ever felt this before: still, with nothing to move toward. She stayed with her hand on the bark and tried to learn what that was.

---

She had read Ambrose's survey the evening before, at Eleni's kitchen table.

Eleni had a copy — he had left her one, for reasons Eleni did not explain and Vesper did not ask about. Three hundred and forty pages, spiral-bound, the cover a photograph he had taken from the ridge above the village: Elathon below, the valley, the olive groves, the ruins of the older settlement visible at the edge of the grove as a slight depression in the ground that only someone who had been looking at it for years would know to see.

She had read it slowly. His handwriting in the margins of his own notes — the small corrections, the questions to himself, the occasional notation that was not archaeological at all but personal: *light here in the afternoon — same as the Mycenaean photographs, someone understood this orientation.* He had spent three years completing it. The methodology was exact. The documentation was thorough. The care on every page was unmistakable — not the care of performance, not the care of someone building a case, but the care of someone paying attention to something they loved.

The final section documented the grove itself. The oldest trees, their approximate ages, their positions. Measurements, photographs, descriptions. He had described the bark of the oldest tree in terms she recognised from the excavation reports — the precision of someone trying to get the thing right, to make the record true to the actual thing. *Surface deeply fissured, grey-brown, with a quality of compression suggesting significant age. The tree stands at the highest point of the grove and is visible from the valley road.*

She had stopped there and looked at the photograph he had taken of this tree — the same tree she was standing at now, in the morning light — and understood, finally, clearly, the thing she had not been able to understand from a distance.

He had not stayed in Elathon because he lacked the ambition to leave. He had stayed because he was capable of the kind of attention that staying required. The kind she had been reaching for across eleven years and five continents and had finally found not in the data but here, in this grove, with her hand on this bark, her feet on this ground.

He had known how to arrive. He had simply done it, every day, for the rest of his life.

---

She stayed in the grove for a long time.

The world continued, as it did. She knew what was happening in it: she had the data, the full picture, the complete shape of the pattern across the whole of recorded history. The working groups were meeting. The journals were processing. Somewhere Maes was in a conference room asking for a higher evidentiary standard. The Downwinders were filing. The black bags were in the fields. The Exclusion Zone was filling with wolves and birch trees and the light through the canopy of a forest growing over abandoned streets.

Brandt had told her: the institution cannot afford to acknowledge what you've found. Ruth had told her: we're not going to stop. Hanna had told her: you can only decide to come home once.

Mori had told her: it cannot tell you what a person should do who is standing inside it.

She was standing inside it. She had always been standing inside it. The data had not exempted her.

What she could do — the only thing available to her that was not another mobilisation, another volume, another response to a methodological objection — was this: be here. In the grove, in December, in the cold, with her hand on the oldest tree in the place she had grown up in and left and spent eleven years not returning to.

The tree had been here for longer than any institution she had studied. It had been here before the soil began dying. It was still here, barely, the roots still working. It had not theorised about the pattern. It had not documented the pattern. It had simply stayed, putting its roots down, year by year, into the ground that was its ground.

She could not save the grove. She knew this. The contamination was in the deeper layers where it did not break down, and the trees that were already grey would not come back, and the lower grove was already gone, and the best science available suggested that the upper grove had perhaps five years. Perhaps less.

She could not save it.

She could be here while it was still here.

She sat down at the base of the oldest tree, her back against the trunk, the ground cold beneath her. From here the village was not visible. The valley was not visible. There was only the grove — the living trees and the dying ones, the morning light coming through the canopy at the angle she knew from Ambrose's photograph, the smell of the soil and the old wood and the cold air off the hills.

She did not open her notebook.

She did not reach for her phone.

She sat with her back against the tree that had been alive for longer than anything she had ever touched and she looked at the grove that was dying and would continue dying and she did not look away.

The light moved through the branches the way it had always moved. A bird she could not see called once from the far end of the grove and was quiet.

She stayed until it was dark. Then she went in.
