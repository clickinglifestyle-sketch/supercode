# THE INTERVAL
## Eleni

After they came in from the grove, Eleni made soup.

Vesper sat at the kitchen table and watched her do it — the particular movement of someone who had cooked in the same kitchen for many years, not looking at anything, reaching without reaching. She knew where everything was without the knowledge being visible. She moved through the kitchen the way Ambrose had moved through the grove, Vesper thought. Without waste. At home.

"I teach at the school in Leonidio," Eleni said, not turning from the stove. "Third and fourth grade. I've been doing it for twenty-two years." She stirred. "I tell you this because I want you to know I am not someone who ran out of options. I'm someone who had options and chose these ones."

Vesper looked at her.

"I'm not defending myself," Eleni said. "I just find it useful to say it directly with people who have been somewhere else for a long time. It stops a certain kind of misunderstanding before it starts."

"I wasn't thinking that."

"No." Eleni set the wooden spoon down. "But you would have wondered at some point. People who leave always wonder about the people who stay — whether we knew something they didn't or missed something they found. The answer is usually neither. We made different choices and then we lived in them."

She brought the bowls to the table and sat.

The kitchen was warm. Through the window the grove was dark, the shapes of the upper trees visible against the sky — the ones that were still holding their canopy faintly present, faintly not.

"He used to come for dinner," Eleni said. "Ambrose. Two or three times a week in the last years. He'd bring things from his garden — onions, tomatoes in season — and I'd make something and we'd sit and talk." She picked up her spoon. "He talked about the survey. The grove, the old settlement, what he was finding in the archive. He talked about the work the way some people talk about their children — proud of it but not possessive. Like it was going to go on without him." She paused. "He talked about you."

Vesper looked at her bowl.

"Not constantly. Not in a way that was difficult. He'd read something you wrote and mention it. Or he'd be working through a problem in the survey and say you'd approach it differently — that you'd go to the documentary record first and he'd go to the soil first and the difference was interesting." She was quiet for a moment. "He was proud of you. I think that was harder for him than being hurt would have been. It's easier to be angry at someone than to be proud of them when they're not there."

Vesper said nothing.

"I was angry," Eleni said. "For the first two years after you left properly — really left — I was angry on his behalf. Which he would have found exasperating if he'd known, because he wasn't angry himself. But I was two years below you in school and I'd watched you both from a slight distance for a long time, and I had an opinion about it." She ate. "And then I thought: he knows what he's doing. He's made his choice. He's not waiting for anything to change. And I stopped being angry because being angry on behalf of someone who has made peace is its own kind of arrogance."

The lentil soup was good — a bay leaf in it, something slow and deep underneath.

"He knew he was ill for about a year," Eleni said. "He didn't tell many people. He told me because I was going to notice anyway — you notice when someone who comes for dinner three times a week starts moving differently." She paused. "He asked me to keep the survey copies safe. He asked me to make sure you knew he'd finished it." She looked at Vesper steadily. "He was happy about it. Not resigned — happy. It was done, and it was good, and it would be in the archive, and that was enough for him. He said: the record is what lasts. The people who made it don't have to."

Outside the window something moved in the upper grove. A branch, or a bird, or the wind from the hillside.

"He left you the cassette," Eleni said. "I'll give it to you when you're ready. Not tonight."

"When will I be ready?"

Eleni considered this seriously, as a genuine question. "Probably when you stop asking." A pause. "You've been here two days. You came in from five months of catastrophe. Give it a week."

Vesper picked up her spoon.

"What is it?" she said. "The tape."

"I don't know. He made it himself — I was there when he dropped it off with me. He said: give this to Vesper when she comes back. I said: what if she doesn't come back. He said: she'll come back." Eleni looked at her. "He said it the way he said things he was certain about. Without making a case for it." She ate. "He was right."

They ate for a while in the quiet that comes after the things that needed saying have been said — not emptiness but something with more room in it.

"The lower grove," Vesper said. "The post. *We are losing them.* Who wrote that?"

"Petros Alexiou. He's ninety-one. His granddaughter types what he dictates. He's been posting since the first trees started going — every time another section dies he puts something on the community page. Small things. Photographs he takes from his bench on the plateia." She paused. "The bench faces the lower grove. He's been sitting there most mornings for thirty years."

"Is he well?"

Eleni looked at her. "He's ninety-one and he sits on a bench watching the trees he grew up under die one by one. Well is a complicated question." She picked up her bowl. "He's still there. He'll be there in the morning, if you want to go."

---

She went in the morning.

The plateia was a small square below the church, a fountain that ran in summer and didn't in December, two benches in the sun. Petros Alexiou occupied one of them with the settled authority of a person who had been occupying it long enough that it had become partially his.

He was very old — the specific fragility of extreme age, but alert, his eyes clear when he looked up at her. He knew her face, or some earlier version of it — the version that had grown up here before it became the face of someone who lived in airports.

"Eleni's friend," he said.

"Eleni's friend." She sat on the other end of the bench. "And Ambrose Katsaros's—" She paused, not finding the word.

"He was a good man," Petros said. "He used to come and sit here. We'd talk about the survey, about what he was finding. He showed me photographs from the archive — pictures of the grove from sixty years ago. More trees. Bigger canopy. Full of it." He turned his face toward the winter sun. "My father took me into the lower grove when I was four years old. I still remember it. The smell — warm in summer, the oil in the bark and something else underneath, something I could never name. I still can't name it."

He was quiet for a moment. Below the plateia the hillside descended toward the valley, the lower grove visible from here — the dead trees standing, still upright, bare in a way that was not seasonal.

"My children don't come here," he said. "They're in Athens, in Kalamata. My grandchildren never saw the lower grove with leaves on it. It was already going when they were small." He didn't say this bitterly. He said it the way a person says something that has long since settled into fact. "You study these things. Why they happen, why things die."

"Yes."

"Does your research say whether it comes back?"

Vesper thought about the Exclusion Zone. The forests returning when the human pressure lifted. The wolves running in the ruins of collective farms. The birch trees growing through the floors of Pripyat.

"If the pressure stops," she said, "things come back. Slowly. Not the same as before. But something comes back."

He looked at this.

"The pressure won't stop," he said.

"Not in time for the trees that are already gone." She said it as plainly as she could. It was what he was asking, and he deserved a plain answer.

He nodded. He had known. He had only wanted someone to say it without flinching.

"My father said the oldest tree in the upper grove was old when his grandfather was young," he said. "So it was always here. It was here before anyone alive now was born. Before their parents were born." He turned his face back to the sun. "Still there?"

"Still there," she said. "A few of them."

He closed his eyes. "Good," he said. "That's something, then."

---

She walked back up through the village slowly, the way you walk through a place you're looking at with the specific attention of someone returning to it after a long time away.

She had spent eleven years studying places at a distance — from satellite images, from archives, from the window of a rental car. She had been very good at it. She had built a methodology from it. She had proved things.

She had not known, walking down this road as a twenty-two-year-old with an admission letter in her bag, that she was leaving the place from which everything she would later study could have been seen most clearly. She had come to understand that elsewhere. She had needed a man in a doorway in Beirut, a farmer on the N29, a woman with a RECOGNITION sign, a hardware store owner in a decontaminated ghost town, an old Ukrainian woman tending her mother's sunflowers in contaminated soil.

She had needed all of them to bring her back here, to this road, to an old man on a bench who had been watching the trees die for eight years and had decided it was something that they were still standing.

The oldest tree was at the top of the slope.

She kept walking toward it.

---

That evening in Eleni's kitchen, she told her about Petros.

"He said: that's something, then," she said. "'Still there' — that's something."

Eleni was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "He says that about a lot of things. It's his way." She poured the tea. "He said it about Ambrose finishing the survey. He said it about the wolves in Chernobyl when I told him about your work. He said it about you coming home." She set the cups down. "I think it's what he has instead of hope. Which is harder to come by but lasts longer."

Vesper wrapped her hands around the cup.

Outside the window the upper grove held in the dark — not healed, not saved, but there. The oldest tree at the top of the slope with its roots down into the soil that was not yet gone, still working, still in the ground that was its ground.

"Eleni," she said.

"Yes."

"I think I'm ready for the tape."

Eleni looked at her for a moment. Then she got up and went to the other room.
